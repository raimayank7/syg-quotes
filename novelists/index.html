Quotes from classic novels

Lines from novels and stories that are still read everywhere, by Louisa May Alcott (1832 to 1888), Charlotte Bronte (1816 to 1855), Emily Bronte (1818 to 1848), Lewis Carroll (1832 to 1898), Arthur Conan Doyle (1859 to 1930), George Eliot (1819 to 1880), Jerome K. Jerome (1859 to 1927), Rudyard Kipling (1865 to 1936), Herman Melville (1819 to 1891), Mary Shelley (1797 to 1851), Robert Louis Stevenson (1850 to 1894) and Leo Tolstoy (1828 to 1910).

Louisa May Alcott

  • I am not afraid of storms, for I am learning how to sail my ship.

    Louisa May Alcott, Little Women (1868)

Charlotte Bronte

  • I am no bird; and no net ensnares me: I am a free human being with an independent will.

    Charlotte Bronte, Jane Eyre (1847)

  • Reader, I married him.

    Charlotte Bronte, Jane Eyre (1847)

Lewis Carroll

  • Curiouser and curiouser!

    Lewis Carroll, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865)

  • Why, sometimes I've believed as many as six impossible things before breakfast.

    Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass (1871)

  • "Begin at the beginning," the King said gravely, "and go on till you come to the end: then stop."

    Lewis Carroll, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865)

  • We're all mad here.

    Lewis Carroll, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865)

Leo Tolstoy

  • Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way.

    Leo Tolstoy, Anna Karenina, translated by Constance Garnett (1901)

George Eliot, Emily Bronte and Mary Shelley

  • What do we live for, if it is not to make life less difficult to each other?

    George Eliot, Middlemarch (1871 to 1872)

  • Whatever our souls are made of, his and mine are the same.

    Emily Bronte, Wuthering Heights (1847)

  • Beware; for I am fearless, and therefore powerful.

    Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818)

Arthur Conan Doyle

  • When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth.

    Arthur Conan Doyle, The Sign of the Four (1890)

  • You see, but you do not observe.

    Arthur Conan Doyle, A Scandal in Bohemia (1891)

  • It is a capital mistake to theorize before one has data.

    Arthur Conan Doyle, A Scandal in Bohemia (1891)

Melville, Stevenson, Kipling and Jerome

  • Call me Ishmael.

    Herman Melville, Moby-Dick (1851)

  • It is not down in any map; true places never are.

    Herman Melville, Moby-Dick (1851)

  • To travel hopefully is a better thing than to arrive.

    Robert Louis Stevenson, Virginibus Puerisque (1881)

  • For the strength of the Pack is the Wolf, and the strength of the Wolf is the Pack.

    Rudyard Kipling, The Second Jungle Book (1895)

  • I like work: it fascinates me. I can sit and look at it for hours.

    Jerome K. Jerome, Three Men in a Boat (1889)

Nothing matches. Try a shorter word, or choose All.

Who says it

  • "I am not afraid of storms, for I am learning how to sail my ship" is Amy March in Little Women.
  • "I am no bird; and no net ensnares me" is Jane Eyre, refusing to stay with Mr Rochester on his terms. "Reader, I married him" opens the novel's last chapter.
  • "Curiouser and curiouser!" is Alice, so surprised that she forgets how to speak good English. "We're all mad here" is the Cheshire Cat. "Begin at the beginning" is the King of Hearts at the trial. The "six impossible things before breakfast" belong to the White Queen in the second book.
  • "Happy families are all alike" is the first sentence of Anna Karenina.
  • "Whatever our souls are made of, his and mine are the same" is Catherine Earnshaw speaking of Heathcliff in Wuthering Heights.
  • "Beware; for I am fearless, and therefore powerful" is the Creature speaking to his maker in Frankenstein.
  • The three detective lines are Sherlock Holmes explaining his method to Dr Watson.
  • "Call me Ishmael" is the first sentence of Moby-Dick.
  • "For the strength of the Pack is the Wolf" comes from the poem "The Law of the Jungle" in The Second Jungle Book.

First lines and last lines

Several entries on this page are the opening or closing words of their books. First lines are useful in a speech because the audience hears the start of a story and wants the rest. If you are writing an essay on a novel, quoting its first sentence and saying what it promises is a strong way to begin.

Using these lines

Amy March's line suits farewells and exam seasons. The Holmes lines suit science days and debates, since they are about evidence. The Kipling line is a ready-made team motto. George Eliot's question from Middlemarch, "What do we live for, if it is not to make life less difficult to each other?", is one of the kindest sentences in English and fits any thank you speech. Give the author and the book each time.

Reading the books

Every novel quoted here is in the public domain and can be read free at Project Gutenberg. Translations are a separate matter: a modern translation of Tolstoy is still in copyright, which is why the line here is given in Constance Garnett's 1901 version.

Good to know

  • "Elementary, my dear Watson" does not appear in any of Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, so it is not listed here.
  • The Tolstoy line is from the English translation by Constance Garnett, published in 1901. Other translators word it a little differently.
